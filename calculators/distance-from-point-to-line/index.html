What is the distance from a point to a line?

The distance from a point to a line is the length of the shortest segment that joins the point to the line. A line contains infinitely many points, so "the distance" could seem ambiguous — but exactly one of those points is closest, and the segment to it always meets the line at a right angle. That is why this quantity is also called the perpendicular distance.

The closest point on the line is called the foot of the perpendicular. If the point already lies on the line, the distance is zero and the foot is the point itself.

What this calculator does

  • Validates your inputs, including the requirement that A and B are not both zero.
  • Computes the numerator ∣Ax1+By1+C∣ and the denominator A2+B2 separately, so you can find exactly where a hand calculation went wrong.
  • Gives the distance as a rounded decimal and, for whole-number and short-decimal inputs, as an exact simplified radical with a rational denominator (for example 655).
  • Finds the foot of the perpendicular and plots the line, the point and the perpendicular segment.
  • Tells you which side of the line the point is on, using the sign of Ax1+By1+C.

Why the shortest path is perpendicular

Let F be the foot of the perpendicular from point P, and let Q be any other point on the line. The points P, F and Q form a right triangle with the right angle at F. The segment PQ is the hypotenuse, and a hypotenuse is always longer than either leg. So PQ>PF for every other choice of Q, which proves PF is the shortest.

The coefficients of the line carry geometric meaning too. The vector (A,B) is a normal vector: it points at right angles to the line. Moving from P straight toward the line means moving along (A,B) or its opposite, which is exactly the direction the distance formula measures.

Which side of the line?

The expression Ax+By+C is zero on the line, positive on one side and negative on the other. The absolute value in the formula throws the sign away so that distance is never negative — but the sign itself tells you which side the point is on, which is useful in graphics and robotics.

Formula breakdown

d=∣Ax1+By1+C∣A2+B2
  • Numerator ∣Ax1+By1+C∣: substitute the point into the left side of the line's equation. If the point were on the line this would be 0; the further away it is, the larger this value.
  • Denominator A2+B2: the length of the normal vector (A,B). Dividing by it removes the effect of how the equation happens to be scaled, turning the numerator into a true distance.

Deriving the formula

Method 1: projection onto the normal vector

Pick any point Q=(xq,yq) on the line, so Axq+Byq+C=0. The vector from Q to P is QP⃗=(x1−xq,  y1−yq). The distance from P to the line is the length of the component of QP⃗ along the unit normal n^=(A,B)A2+B2:

d=∣QP⃗⋅n^∣=∣A(x1−xq)+B(y1−yq)∣A2+B2=∣Ax1+By1−(Axq+Byq)∣A2+B2.

Because Q is on the line, Axq+Byq=−C. Substituting gives the formula d=∣Ax1+By1+C∣A2+B2. The choice of Q did not matter, which is reassuring.

Method 2: find the foot of the perpendicular

The perpendicular through P runs in the direction (A,B), so its points are (x1−At,  y1−Bt). Substituting into the line equation and solving for t:

A(x1−At)+B(y1−Bt)+C=0  ⟹  t=Ax1+By1+CA2+B2.

The distance is then ∣t∣A2+B2, which simplifies to the same formula. This method also produces the foot F=(x1−At,  y1−Bt), which the calculator reports.

Complete worked example

Problem

Find the distance from the point (4,−1) to the line 3x+4y−2=0, and find the closest point on the line. (This is the calculator's Load example problem.)

Step 1 — Identify the values

x1=4, y1=−1, A=3, B=4, C=−2.

Step 2 — Numerator

∣Ax1+By1+C∣=∣3(4)+4(−1)+(−2)∣=∣12−4−2∣=∣6∣=6

Step 3 — Denominator

A2+B2=32+42=9+16=25=5

Step 4 — Distance

d=65=1.2

Step 5 — Foot of the perpendicular

t=625=0.24,F=(4−3(0.24),  −1−4(0.24))=(3.28,  −1.96)

Check:

3(3.28)+4(−1.96)−2=9.84−7.84−2=0

So F lies on the line. The distance from (4,−1) to (3.28,−1.96) is 0.722+0.962=1.44=1.2, which agrees.

Step-by-step example starting from y = mx + b

Problem

How far is the point (−2,3) from the line y=2x+1?

Step 1 — Rewrite the line in general form

Subtract y from both sides: 0=2x−y+1, that is 2x−y+1=0. So A=2, B=−1, C=1.

Step 2 — Substitute

d=∣2(−2)+(−1)(3)+1∣22+(−1)2=∣−4−3+1∣5=65

Step 3 — Rationalise the denominator

d=65⋅55=655≈2.683282

The numerator before the absolute value was −6, which is negative: the point is on the side of the line where 2x−y+1<0, which here is above the line.

Practical applications

  • Engineering mechanics. The moment (torque) of a force about a point equals the force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the point to the force's line of action — exactly this distance.
  • Surveying and road design. The offset of a survey point from a straight boundary or road centreline is a point-to-line distance in local coordinates.
  • Manufacturing and CAD. Checking whether a measured point on a part is within tolerance of a straight edge, or computing clearances in a drawing.
  • Robotics and navigation. Path-following controllers measure how far a robot has drifted from a straight planned path, and the sign tells it which way to steer.
  • Computer graphics. Deciding whether a mouse click is close enough to "hit" a drawn line, and clipping shapes against edges.
  • Data science. A support vector machine's margin uses the higher-dimensional version, ∣w⋅x+b∣/∥w∥, to measure how far a data point is from a separating boundary.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

  1. Not rearranging to general form first. The formula needs Ax+By+C=0. With 3x+4y=2, the constant is C=−2, not 2.
  2. Sign errors when moving terms. From y=2x+1, the coefficient of y becomes B=−1. Keep every sign attached to its term.
  3. Dropping the absolute value. A negative answer is a signal of a missing absolute value, not a negative distance.
  4. Forgetting the square root. The denominator is A2+B2, not A2+B2 or A+B.
  5. Squaring negatives incorrectly. (−4)2=16. Typing −42 into many calculators gives −16; use brackets.
  6. Measuring vertically. The vertical gap between the point and the line, ∣y1−(mx1+b)∣, is only the distance when the line is horizontal. For a sloped line the perpendicular distance is always smaller.
  7. Worrying about scaling. Multiplying the whole equation by a nonzero number (for example writing 6x+8y−4=0 instead of 3x+4y−2=0) does not change the distance: numerator and denominator scale together.

Frequently asked questions

Can the distance from a point to a line be negative?

No. Distance is always zero or positive, which is why the formula takes the absolute value of Ax₁ + By₁ + C. The sign of that expression before the absolute value only tells you which side of the line the point lies on.

What does a distance of zero mean?

The point lies on the line: substituting its coordinates makes Ax + By + C equal to zero. The foot of the perpendicular is then the point itself.

How do I use the formula if my line is written as y = mx + b?

Move every term to one side to get mx − y + b = 0. Then A = m, B = −1 and C = b. For example, y = −3x + 5 becomes −3x − y + 5 = 0, or equivalently 3x + y − 5 = 0.

Why can A and B not both be zero?

If A = B = 0 the equation becomes C = 0, which has no x or y in it. It is either true everywhere or nowhere, so it does not describe a line, and the denominator √(A² + B²) would be zero.

How do I find the distance between two parallel lines?

Write both lines with the same A and B, as Ax + By + C₁ = 0 and Ax + By + C₂ = 0. The distance is |C₁ − C₂| divided by √(A² + B²). Alternatively, pick any point on one line and use this calculator to find its distance to the other line.

Does multiplying the line equation by a number change the answer?

No. Multiplying the equation by a nonzero number k multiplies the numerator by |k| and the denominator by |k|, so the distance stays the same. That is why the calculator gives the same result for 3x + 4y − 2 = 0 and 6x + 8y − 4 = 0.

What is the foot of the perpendicular?

It is the point on the line closest to the given point — where the perpendicular segment meets the line. The calculator gives it as F = (x₁ − At, y₁ − Bt) with t = (Ax₁ + By₁ + C) / (A² + B²).

Is there a similar formula in three dimensions?

Yes, for a plane: the distance from (x₁, y₁, z₁) to Ax + By + Cz + D = 0 is |Ax₁ + By₁ + Cz₁ + D| divided by √(A² + B² + C²). The distance from a point to a line in 3D uses the cross product instead.
