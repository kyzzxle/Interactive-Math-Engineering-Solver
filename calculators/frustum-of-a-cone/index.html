What is a frustum of a cone?

A frustum of a cone — also called a truncated cone — is the solid that remains when a cone is sliced by a plane parallel to its base and the small top piece (including the tip) is removed. The word frustum is Latin for "a piece broken off".

The result has two parallel circular faces of different sizes joined by a smooth, sloping curved surface. A drinking cup, a flower pot, a lampshade and a concrete pier footing are all everyday frustums. This page deals with the right circular frustum, where the line joining the centres of the two circles (the axis) is perpendicular to both of them.

What this calculator does

Frustum problems usually give three measurements and ask for one or more of the others. The calculator handles the two most common situations:

  • You know the two radii and the perpendicular height — it finds the slant height first.
  • You know the two radii and the slant height — it finds the perpendicular height first, after checking that such a frustum can exist.

It then reports the lateral (curved) surface area, the areas of both circular bases, the total surface area and the volume. When your inputs are whole numbers or short decimals, the answers are also given in exact form (such as 228π or 25), which is what most textbooks and exams expect, alongside a rounded decimal. The step-by-step solution uses your own numbers, so you can compare it line by line with your written work.

The geometry behind the formulas

Almost everything about a frustum follows from one picture. Cut the frustum straight down through its axis and the cut face is an isosceles trapezoid: its parallel sides are the two diameters 2r and 2R, its height is h, and its two slanted sides each have length s.

Now drop a vertical line from the edge of the top circle down to the bottom base. It lands a horizontal distance R−r inside the bottom edge, and it creates a right triangle with:

  • a vertical leg equal to the height h,
  • a horizontal leg equal to the difference of the radii R−r, and
  • a hypotenuse equal to the slant height s.

That triangle is why the Pythagorean theorem links h, s and R−r. A second key idea is similarity: the original full cone and the small cone that was cut off have exactly the same shape, so all their lengths are in the ratio r:R. The area and volume formulas below come from subtracting the small cone from the large one.

Two heights, two jobs

The perpendicular height h measures how tall the solid is, so it controls the volume. The slant height s measures the length of the sloping side, so it controls the curved surface area. Mixing them up is the most common frustum error.

Deriving the formulas

Slant height and height

In the right triangle from the cross-section, the legs are h and R−r and the hypotenuse is s. The Pythagorean theorem gives s2=h2+(R−r)2, which rearranges to either formula in the table.

Volume as the difference of two cones

Imagine extending the sloping sides upward until they meet at the apex. Let the full cone have height H. The removed top cone has height H−h. Because the two cones are similar, their heights are in the same ratio as their radii:

H−hH=rR⟹H=RhR−r,H−h=rhR−r.

Subtract the small cone's volume from the large cone's volume (Vcone=13π(radius)2(height)):

V=π3[R2⋅RhR−r−r2⋅rhR−r]=πh3⋅R3−r3R−r.

The difference of cubes factors as R3−r3=(R−r)(R2+Rr+r2), so the R−r cancels and

V=πh3(R2+Rr+r2).

Notice that the final formula still works when r=R (it gives πR2h, the cylinder) even though the derivation divided by R−r.

Lateral area as the difference of two cones

A cone with radius ρ and slant length ℓ has curved area πρℓ. The large cone has slant length L and the small cone L−s. By similarity (L−s)/L=r/R, so L=RsR−r and L−s=rsR−r. Subtracting:

LSA=πRL−πr(L−s)=πs (R2−r2)R−r=π(R+r) s.

A helpful way to remember it: π(R+r) is the average of the two circumferences 2πR and 2πr, and multiplying by the slant height is like unrolling the curved surface into a strip of that average length and width s.

A calculus check

If you have studied integration, slice the frustum into thin discs. At height y above the large base the radius shrinks linearly from R to r: ρ(y)=R−(R−r) y/h. Adding up the discs,

V=∫0hπ(R−(R−r) yh)2dy=πh3(R2+Rr+r2),

which confirms the geometric result.

Complete worked example

Problem

A plant pot has the shape of a frustum. The top (smaller) radius is 6 cm, the bottom (larger) radius is 9 cm, and the perpendicular height is 4 cm. Find the slant height, the lateral surface area, the total surface area and the volume. (This is the calculator's Load example problem, so you can check each line against it.)

Step 1 — List the given values

r=6 cm, R=9 cm, h=4 cm, so R−r=3 cm.

Step 2 — Slant height

s=h2+(R−r)2=42+32=16+9=25=5 cm

Step 3 — Lateral surface area

LSA=π(R+r) s=π(9+6)(5)=75π≈235.62 cm2

Step 4 — Areas of the bases

πR2=π(9)2=81π,πr2=π(6)2=36π

Step 5 — Total surface area

TSA=75π+81π+36π=192π≈603.19 cm2

Step 6 — Volume

V=πh3(R2+Rr+r2)=π(4)3(81+54+36)=4π(171)3=228π≈716.28 cm3

Answer

s=5 cm, LSA=75π≈235.62 cm², TSA=192π≈603.19 cm², and V=228π≈716.28 cm³. Since 1000 cm³ = 1 litre, the pot holds about 0.72 litres of soil. A real pot is open at the top, so the material needed for its walls and floor would be LSA+πR2=156π≈490.09 cm².

Step-by-step example when the slant height is given

Engineering drawings of sheet-metal parts often give the slant length rather than the height, because that is the length of metal along the side. Here the height must be found first.

Problem

A steel hopper is a frustum with radii 1.0 m and 0.2 m and a slant height of 1.0 m. How much grain can it hold, and how much sheet steel forms its sloping wall?

Step 1 — Assign the radii

The larger radius is R=1.0 m and the smaller is r=0.2 m (even if the hopper is mounted with the wide end on top). Then R−r=0.8 m.

Step 2 — Check that the frustum can exist

The slant height is the hypotenuse, so it must be longer than R−r. Here 1.0>0.8, so the shape is possible.

Step 3 — Perpendicular height

h=s2−(R−r)2=1.02−0.82=1−0.64=0.36=0.6 m

Step 4 — Volume (capacity)

V=π(0.6)3(1.02+(1.0)(0.2)+0.22)=0.2π (1.24)=0.248π≈0.779 m3

Step 5 — Sloping wall (lateral area)

LSA=π(1.0+0.2)(1.0)=1.2π≈3.770 m2

Answer

The hopper holds about 0.779 m³ (roughly 779 litres), and its sloping wall uses about 3.77 m² of steel, before allowing for overlaps, seams and waste.

Practical applications in engineering and construction

  • Containers and capacity. Buckets, pails, cups, tubs and planters are frustums. The volume formula gives their capacity, and surveyors use the same idea to estimate tapered tanks.
  • Hoppers, chutes and funnels. Storage hoppers for grain, cement or aggregate taper to an outlet. Designers need the volume for capacity and the lateral area to estimate steel plate.
  • Concrete footings and piers. Tapered (frustum-shaped) footings and column bases spread a load onto the soil. The volume tells a contractor how many cubic metres of concrete to order.
  • Sheet-metal and duct work. Concentric reducers join round ducts or pipes of different diameters. Their flat pattern is a ring-shaped sector whose area equals the frustum's lateral area.
  • Earthworks and stockpiles. A conical stockpile with its top flattened, or a circular pit with sloping sides, is approximately a frustum, so the formula gives a quick estimate of excavated or stored volume.
  • Lighting, acoustics and product design. Lampshades, loudspeaker cones and nozzles are frustums; their surface area determines material cost and their dimensions affect how light or sound spreads.

Open or closed?

Decide which faces the real object actually has. A closed tank uses the total surface area; an open bucket uses the lateral area plus one base; a pipe reducer or lampshade uses the lateral area alone.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

  1. Using diameters instead of radii. Problems often state diameters. Halve them first — using a diameter doubles every length and multiplies the volume by eight.
  2. Swapping slant height and height. Volume uses h; the curved area uses s. If a problem gives only one of them, find the other with the Pythagorean relationship.
  3. Forgetting the middle term. The volume bracket is R2+Rr+r2, not R2+r2. Dropping Rr underestimates the volume.
  4. Averaging instead of using the formula. Treating the frustum as a cylinder of average radius gives π(7.5)2(4)=225π for the worked example, and averaging the two end areas gives 234π. Both are wrong; the true volume is 228π. (The "average end area" method common in earthwork estimates always slightly overestimates the volume of a frustum.)
  5. Rounding too early. If s is irrational, keep it as a surd such as 25 or keep several decimal places until the final step.
  6. Mixing units. Convert every length to the same unit before substituting. Areas come out in square units and volumes in cubic units.
  7. Assuming any slant height works. The slant height must be longer than R−r. If it is not, no such frustum exists — the calculator will flag this rather than return a meaningless answer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a cone and a frustum of a cone?

A cone has one circular base and comes to a point (the apex). A frustum is a cone with its tip cut off by a plane parallel to the base, so it has two parallel circular faces and no apex. A cone is the special case of a frustum whose top radius is zero.

Can the top radius be zero?

Yes. With r = 0 the frustum formulas reduce to the cone formulas: the volume becomes one third of π R² h and the lateral area becomes π R s. The calculator accepts r = 0 and tells you that the solid is a complete cone.

How do I find the volume if I only know the slant height?

First find the perpendicular height with h = √(s² − (R − r)²), then use the usual volume formula. Choose "Slant height s" in the calculator and it does both steps for you.

Why does the volume formula use the height and not the slant height?

Volume depends on how far apart the two faces are, measured straight across. The slant height is the length of a sloping side, which is always longer than the gap between the faces, so using it would overestimate the volume.

How do I find the capacity of a bucket in litres?

Measure the radii and the height in centimetres, calculate the volume in cubic centimetres and divide by 1000, because 1 litre equals 1000 cm³. If you measure in metres, multiply the cubic metres by 1000 instead.

What happens if both radii are equal?

The solid is a cylinder. The slant height equals the height, the lateral area becomes 2πRh and the volume becomes πR²h. The frustum formulas give these results automatically.

Does this calculator work for an oblique (tilted) frustum?

Only partly. The volume formula still holds for an oblique frustum as long as h is the perpendicular distance between the faces, by Cavalieri's principle. The slant height and lateral area formulas apply only to a right frustum, where every slant line has the same length.

Why does the calculator show both an exact answer and a decimal?

Exact answers such as 228π or 2√5 contain no rounding error and are what most teachers expect. Decimals are easier to use in practical work. The decimal is marked as approximate whenever it has been rounded.
