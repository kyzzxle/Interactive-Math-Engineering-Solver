What are permutations and combinations?

Permutations and combinations are the two basic tools of combinatorics, the mathematics of counting. Both answer the question "how many ways can I choose r items from n different items, without using any item twice?" They differ in one detail:

  • A permutation is an ordered arrangement. Choosing Ana then Ben is different from choosing Ben then Ana.
  • A combination is an unordered selection. {Ana, Ben} is the same group however you list it.

Because each group of r items can be ordered in several ways, there are always at least as many permutations as combinations.

What this calculator does

Order versus selection

Everything rests on the multiplication principle: if one decision can be made in a ways and a second, independent decision in b ways, the pair of decisions can be made in a×b ways.

Suppose 10 students run for three different offices — president, vice-president and secretary. There are 10 choices for president, then 9 left for vice-president, then 8 for secretary: 10×9×8=720 outcomes. Order matters, because the positions are different.

Now suppose the same 10 students form a three-person committee with no titles. Each committee, say {Ana, Ben, Cy}, was counted 3!=6 times in the 720 above (once for each way of assigning the three titles). Dividing removes the duplicates: 720÷6=120 committees.

The test

Imagine swapping two of the chosen items. If the outcome changes, order matters: use a permutation. If nothing changes, use a combination.

Formula breakdown

P(n,r)=n!(n−r)!=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯(n−r+1) C(n,r)=(nr)=n!r! (n−r)!=P(n,r)r!

The factorial n! is the product of all whole numbers from n down to 1, for example 5!=5×4×3×2×1=120. By definition 0!=1.

You will see several notations for the same quantities: P(n,r), nPr, nPr and Prn for permutations; C(n,r), nCr, nCr and (nr) ("n choose r") for combinations. The last one is also called a binomial coefficient because it gives the coefficients in the expansion of (a+b)n.

Two identities are worth remembering:

C(n,r)=C(n,n−r)andP(n,r)=C(n,r)×r!.

The first says that choosing which r items to take is the same as choosing which n−r to leave behind.

Where the formulas come from

Permutations

Fill r positions in order. The first position has n candidates, the second n−1, and so on, until the r-th position has n−r+1. By the multiplication principle,

P(n,r)=n(n−1)⋯(n−r+1).

Multiplying and dividing by (n−r)! completes the factorial on top:

P(n,r)=n(n−1)⋯(n−r+1) ⋅ (n−r)!(n−r)!=n!(n−r)!.

Combinations

Every unordered group of r items can be arranged in r! orders, and every ordered arrangement comes from exactly one group. So the permutations are the combinations, each counted r! times:

P(n,r)=C(n,r)⋅r!⟹C(n,r)=n!r! (n−r)!.

Why 0! = 1

There is exactly one way to arrange zero objects — do nothing. The definition also keeps the formulas consistent: P(n,n)=n!/0! must equal n!, and C(n,0)=n!/(0! n!) must equal 1.

Complete worked example

Problem

A class has 10 students. (a) In how many ways can a president, a vice-president and a secretary be elected? (b) In how many ways can a three-person committee be formed? (This is the calculator's Load example.)

n=10 students, r=3 chosen.

(a) 720 ways to fill the three offices. (b) 120 possible committees. Check: 120×3!=720.

Practical applications

  • Probability. Many probabilities are "favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes", and both are usually counted with combinations — for instance, the chance of one particular lottery ticket winning a 6-from-49 draw is 1/13,983,816.
  • Reliability engineering. A "k-out-of-n" system works if at least k of its n components work. Its reliability formula sums terms containing C(n,i).
  • Quality control. When inspectors test a sample from a batch, the probability of finding a given number of defective parts (the hypergeometric distribution) is built from combinations.
  • Network design. Connecting every pair of n nodes directly needs C(n,2)=n(n−1)/2 links, which explains why fully connected networks become expensive quickly.
  • Scheduling and tournaments. A round-robin league with n teams has C(n,2) matches; ordered fixtures (home and away) number P(n,2).
  • Security. Password and key-space sizes measure how many guesses a brute-force attack might need.
  • Algebra. The binomial theorem (a+b)n=∑r=0nC(n,r) an−rbr uses combinations as its coefficients — the numbers in Pascal's triangle.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

  1. Using a permutation when order does not matter (or the reverse). Apply the swap test before calculating.
  2. Applying these formulas when items can repeat. PIN codes, licence plates and dice rolls usually allow repetition; use nr for those.
  3. Ignoring identical items. Arrangements of the letters of LEVEL are not 5!, because the two L's and the two E's are indistinguishable. Divide by the factorial of each repeat count: 5!/(2! 2!)=30.
  4. Thinking 0! = 0. It equals 1, which is why C(n,0)=C(n,n)=1.
  5. Computing giant factorials directly. 100! has 158 digits and overflows most calculators. Cancel (n−r)! first, as the calculator does.
  6. Letting r exceed n. You cannot choose 7 items from 5 without repetition. The count is 0, and the factorial formula is undefined because (n−r)! would need a negative factorial.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a permutation and a combination?

A permutation counts ordered arrangements, so ABC and CBA are different. A combination counts unordered selections, so ABC and CBA are the same group. For the same n and r, the number of permutations is the number of combinations multiplied by r!.

Why is 0! equal to 1?

There is exactly one way to arrange nothing. Defining 0! = 1 also keeps the formulas consistent, so that P(n, n) = n! and C(n, 0) = 1.

Can r be larger than n?

Not without repetition. There are zero ways to choose more items than exist, and the factorial formula is undefined in that case, so the calculator asks you to correct the input.

How does the calculator handle very large numbers?

It uses JavaScript BigInt, which stores whole numbers of any size exactly, and it multiplies only the factors that survive cancellation. Results with up to 200,000 digits are shown exactly with a button to view or copy every digit. Larger results are shown in scientific notation and labelled as approximations.

Why does C(n, r) equal C(n, n − r)?

Every choice of r items to keep is also a choice of n − r items to leave out, so the two counts match. For example, C(10, 7) = C(10, 3) = 120.

How do I count arrangements when items can be repeated?

If order matters and repetition is allowed, there are n to the power r arrangements. If order does not matter and repetition is allowed, there are C(n + r − 1, r) selections. These are separate formulas from the ones on this page.

How many ways can n people sit around a round table?

(n − 1)! ways, if rotations of the same seating count as identical. Fix one person's seat to remove the rotations, then arrange the remaining n − 1 people in a row.

What do nPr and nCr mean on a scientific calculator?

They are the permutation and combination functions. Type n, press nPr or nCr, type r, and press equals. They give the same values as P(n, r) and C(n, r) on this page, though a handheld calculator switches to rounded scientific notation for large results.
