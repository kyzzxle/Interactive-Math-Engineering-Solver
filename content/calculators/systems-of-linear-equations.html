Systems of linear equations

A system of linear equations is a set of equations that must all be true at once. With two unknowns, each equation is a line and the solution is where the lines meet; with three unknowns each equation is a plane. This solver handles systems of two equations in \(x, y\) or three in \(x, y, z\), using the elimination method written as row operations, with exact fractions throughout.

Try changing the input

Make the second equation a multiple of the first (for example double every number). The two lines become one, and the solver reports infinitely many solutions in terms of a parameter \(t\).

Method

Write the coefficients as an augmented matrix and use three operations that never change the solutions:

  1. Swap two equations: \(R_i \leftrightarrow R_j\).
  2. Multiply an equation by a non-zero number: \(R_i \to kR_i\).
  3. Add a multiple of one equation to another: \(R_i \to R_i + kR_j\) (this is "elimination").
\[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{array}\right] \;\longrightarrow\; \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 0 & x \\ 0 & 1 & y \end{array}\right] \]

The outcome is one of three cases: a unique solution, no solution (a row reads \(0 = k\) with \(k \ne 0\)), or infinitely many solutions (a free variable remains).

Worked example

Solve \(2x + 3y = 12\) and \(x - y = 1\)

Step 1 — Eliminate x

Multiply the second equation by 2 and subtract it from the first:

\[ (2x + 3y) - (2x - 2y) = 12 - 2 \;\Rightarrow\; 5y = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 2 \]

Step 2 — Back-substitute

\[ x - 2 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 3 \]

Step 3 — Check

\(2(3) + 3(2) = 12\) ✓ and \(3 - 2 = 1\) ✓.

Assumptions, domain and rounding

  • Two equations in two unknowns or three equations in three unknowns. Type 0 for a missing term.
  • Coefficients can be integers, decimals or fractions; results are exact fractions.
  • For larger systems, or to use Cramer's rule or the inverse matrix, use the matrix methods calculator.
