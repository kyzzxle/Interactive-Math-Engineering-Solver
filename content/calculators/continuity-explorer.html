What the continuity explorer does

A function is continuous at a point if you can draw its graph through that point without lifting your pencil. Formally, three things must hold: f(a) exists, the limit exists, and the two are equal.

When one fails, the type of failure names the discontinuity: a removable one (a hole), a jump (different one-sided limits), an infinite one (a vertical asymptote), or an oscillating one.

Try it

In the explorer, make the two pieces meet and set f(1) to the same height: the function becomes continuous.

Formulas and variables

\[ f \text{ is continuous at } a \iff \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \]
SymbolMeaning
\(f(a)\)Value at the point
\(\lim_{x \to a^\pm} f(x)\)One-sided limits

Removable: limits agree but differ from f(a) (or f(a) is missing). Jump: finite one-sided limits that differ. Infinite: at least one side grows without bound.

Worked example

Is (x² − 1)/(x − 1) continuous at x = 1?

Step 1 — f(1)

\(f(1) = \tfrac{0}{0}\) is not defined.

Step 2 — Limit

\[ \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 2 \]

Step 3 — Verdict

The limit exists but f(1) does not: a removable discontinuity (a hole at (1, 2)).

Domain, special cases and common mistakes

  • One formula, or a piecewise function made of a left formula, a value at a and a right formula.
  • One-sided limits are estimated numerically.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking “not defined at a” always means “no limit at a”.
  • Checking only one side of a piecewise function.
