What the compound interest calculator does

With compound interest, the interest earned in each period is added to the balance, so the next period's interest is calculated on a larger amount. Money grows exponentially instead of in a straight line. This is the idea behind the time value of money: an amount today is worth more than the same amount later, because it can earn interest in the meantime.

The calculator finds the future worth \(F\), the present worth \(P\), the nominal rate \(r\) or the number of years \(n\), for interest compounded annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly, daily or continuously. It also gives the effective annual rate, a year-by-year balance table and a graph comparing compound with simple interest.

Try it

In the explorer, keep the rate and years fixed and change the compounding from annually to continuously. The future worth rises, but by less each time: compounding more often has diminishing returns.

Formulas and variables

\[ F = P\,(1 + i)^{N}, \qquad i = \frac{r}{m}, \qquad N = m\,n \]
\[ P = \frac{F}{(1 + i)^{N}} \qquad F_{\text{continuous}} = P\,e^{r n} \]
SymbolMeaning
\(P\)Present worth (principal)
\(F\)Future worth after \(n\) years
\(r\)Nominal annual rate, as a decimal
\(m\)Compounding periods per year (1, 2, 4, 12, 365)
\(i\)Interest rate per period
\(n,\ N\)Years, and the total number of compounding periods

The factor \((1 + i)^N\) is often written \((F/P, i, N)\) in engineering economy tables, and its reciprocal \((P/F, i, N)\).

Worked example

100,000 invested at 6% compounded quarterly for 5 years

Step 1: Rate per period and number of periods

\[ i = \frac{0.06}{4} = 0.015, \qquad N = 4 \times 5 = 20 \]

Step 2: Future worth

\[ F = 100{,}000\,(1.015)^{20} \approx 100{,}000 \times 1.346855 \approx 134{,}685.50 \]

Step 3: Compare

Simple interest at 6% would give \(100{,}000\,(1 + 0.06 \times 5) = 130{,}000\). The extra 4,685.50 is interest earned on interest.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The rate stays constant for the whole period and there are no deposits or withdrawals. For a series of equal payments, use the annuity calculator.
  • Interest is added only at the end of each period, so a fractional number of periods (for example 5.45 years compounded annually) is an approximation. The balance table shows whole years.
  • To compare rates with different compounding, convert them with the effective interest rate calculator.
  • Amounts are rounded to two decimal places and can be in any currency.
