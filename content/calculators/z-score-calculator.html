What is a z-score?

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean. It puts measurements from different scales on a common footing — a test score of 82 and a height of 160 cm can both be described as "1.5 standard deviations above average" or "below average". If the data are normally distributed, the z-score also gives the percentile: the proportion of values below it.

Try changing the input

Keep x fixed and increase σ: the z-score shrinks towards 0, because the same distance from the mean is less unusual when the data are more spread out.

Formulas and variables

\[ z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \qquad x = \mu + z\sigma \qquad \text{percentile} = \Phi(z) \]
SymbolMeaning
\(x\)The value being standardised.
\(\mu, \sigma\)Mean and standard deviation of the distribution (\(\sigma > 0\)).
\(\Phi(z)\)Standard normal cumulative probability: the area to the left of \(z\).

The 68–95–99.7 rule: in a normal distribution about 68% of values have \(|z| < 1\), 95% have \(|z| < 2\) and 99.7% have \(|z| < 3\).

Worked example

A test score of 82 when \(\mu = 70\) and \(\sigma = 8\)

Step 1 — Standardise

\[ z = \frac{82 - 70}{8} = 1.5 \]

Step 2 — Percentile

\[ \Phi(1.5) \approx 0.9332 \]

The score is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean, higher than about 93% of scores (if the scores are approximately normal).

Assumptions, domain and rounding

  • The z-score itself needs no assumptions, but converting it to a percentile assumes the data are approximately normally distributed.
  • \(\sigma\) must be positive.
  • Probabilities use an accurate normal CDF (about 15 significant digits) and are rounded to 6 decimal places.
