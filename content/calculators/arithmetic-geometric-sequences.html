What are arithmetic and geometric sequences?

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers. In an arithmetic sequence you add the same common difference \(d\) each time: 3, 7, 11, 15, … (\(d = 4\)). In a geometric sequence you multiply by the same common ratio \(r\): 3, 1.5, 0.75, … (\(r = \tfrac{1}{2}\)). Both have simple formulas for the \(n\)th term and for the sum of the first \(n\) terms, and a geometric series with \(|r| \lt 1\) even has a finite infinite sum.

Try changing the input

Make the ratio \(r = 2\) instead of \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). The terms grow without limit and the infinite sum no longer exists.

The ideas behind it

Reaching the \(n\)th term takes \(n - 1\) steps from the first, so \(a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d\) or \(a_n = a_1r^{n - 1}\).

Arithmetic sums. Pair the first and last terms, the second and second-to-last, and so on: every pair adds to \(a_1 + a_n\), and there are \(\tfrac{n}{2}\) pairs.

Geometric sums. Multiply the sum by \(r\) and subtract it from the original: everything cancels except the first and last pieces.

Infinite sums

When \(|r| \lt 1\), \(r^n \to 0\), so \(S_n \to \tfrac{a_1}{1 - r}\). For \(3 + 1.5 + 0.75 + \dots\) the total is \(\tfrac{3}{1 - 0.5} = 6\).

Variables explained

\(a_n, S_n\)The nth term and the sum of the first n terms.

Deriving the geometric sum

Write \(S_n = a_1 + a_1r + \dots + a_1r^{n - 1}\) and multiply by \(r\): \(rS_n = a_1r + a_1r^2 + \dots + a_1r^n\). Subtract:

\[ S_n - rS_n = a_1 - a_1r^n \;\Rightarrow\; S_n = \frac{a_1(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}. \]

The arithmetic sum comes from writing the series forwards and backwards and adding: each of the \(n\) columns adds to \(a_1 + a_n\), so \(2S_n = n(a_1 + a_n)\).

Complete worked example

Problem

For the arithmetic sequence 3, 7, 11, …, find the 10th term and the sum of the first 10 terms.

Step 1: Identify

\(a_1 = 3\), \(d = 4\), \(n = 10\).

Step 2: 10th term

\[ a_{10} = 3 + 9(4) = 39 \]

Step 3: Sum

\[ S_{10} = \frac{10(3 + 39)}{2} = 210 \]

Check

\(\tfrac{10}{2}\left[2(3) + 9(4)\right] = 5(42) = 210\). ✓

Second worked example: a geometric series

Problem

For \(3, \tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{3}{4}, \dots\), find the 6th term, the sum of 6 terms and the infinite sum.

Step 1: Identify

\(a_1 = 3\), \(r = \tfrac{1}{2}\).

Step 2: 6th term and sum

\[ a_6 = 3\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^5 = \tfrac{3}{32}, \qquad S_6 = \frac{3\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{64}\right)}{1 - \tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{189}{32} \approx 5.906 \]

Step 3: Infinite sum

\[ S_\infty = \frac{3}{1 - \tfrac{1}{2}} = 6 \]

The partial sums creep up towards 6 but never pass it.

Practical applications

  • Finance: simple interest and straight-line depreciation are arithmetic; compound interest and annuities are geometric (see the annuity calculator).
  • Construction: seats in rows that each have a few more than the last.
  • Physics: a bouncing ball that rises to a fixed fraction of its previous height travels a geometric total distance.
  • Population and decay: growth or decay by a constant factor each period.

Common mistakes

  1. Using \(n\) instead of \(n - 1\) in the nth-term formula.
  2. Mixing up d and r. Check whether the terms differ by a fixed amount or a fixed factor.
  3. Using the infinite-sum formula when \(|r| \ge 1\). The series then has no finite sum.
  4. Sign of a negative ratio. With \(r = -\tfrac{1}{2}\) the terms alternate in sign.

Assumptions and limitations

  • \(n\) is a whole number from 1 to 100,000. Terms and sums are exact fractions when they are not too long, otherwise decimals.
  • For other kinds of sequences, see recurrence relations.

Frequently asked questions

How do I tell if a sequence is arithmetic or geometric?

Subtract neighbouring terms: a constant difference means arithmetic. Divide them: a constant ratio means geometric.

What is the formula for the nth term?

Arithmetic: aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1)d. Geometric: aₙ = a₁·rⁿ⁻¹.

When does an infinite geometric series have a sum?

When the ratio is between −1 and 1. The sum is then a₁ / (1 − r).

How do I find the sum of an arithmetic sequence?

Multiply the number of terms by the average of the first and last terms: Sₙ = n(a₁ + aₙ)/2.
