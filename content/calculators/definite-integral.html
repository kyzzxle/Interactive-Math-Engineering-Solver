Definite integrals

The definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) adds up \(f(x)\) over the interval from \(a\) to \(b\): geometrically, it is the signed area between the graph and the x-axis. This calculator uses two clearly labelled methods: an exact answer from the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus whenever it can find an antiderivative, and a numerical answer from adaptive Simpson's rule otherwise. The exact answer is always cross-checked numerically.

Try changing the input

Integrate \(\sin x\) from 0 to \(\pi\) (area 2), then from 0 to \(2\pi\): the result is 0 because the area below the axis cancels the area above it.

Formulas and methods

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a), \qquad F'(x) = f(x) \]

Adaptive Simpson's rule approximates \(f\) by parabolas on small sub-intervals,

\[ \int_{x_0}^{x_2} f(x)\,dx \approx \frac{h}{3}\left[f(x_0) + 4f(x_1) + f(x_2)\right], \]

and keeps halving any sub-interval whose estimate is not yet accurate, until the overall error is about \(10^{-11}\).

Worked example

\(\int_0^3 (x^2 + 1)\,dx\)

Step 1 — Antiderivative

\[ F(x) = \frac{x^3}{3} + x \]

Step 2 — Evaluate

\[ F(3) - F(0) = (9 + 3) - 0 = 12 \]

Assumptions, domain and rounding

  • \(f\) must be defined and finite everywhere on \([a, b]\). Improper integrals (an infinite limit, or a vertical asymptote inside the interval, as with \(\frac{1}{x}\) on \([-1, 1]\)) are rejected.
  • Limits may be expressions such as pi/2 or e.
  • Polynomials with rational limits give exact fractions. Other exact-method results and all numerical results are decimals; numerical values are shown to 9 decimal places.
