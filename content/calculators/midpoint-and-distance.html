What are the midpoint and the distance between two points?

Given two points \(P_1(x_1, y_1)\) and \(P_2(x_2, y_2)\) in the coordinate plane, the midpoint is the point exactly halfway between them and the distance is the length of the segment joining them. They are among the first tools of analytic geometry: the midpoint finds centres and bisectors, and the distance formula measures lengths, radii and perimeters.

Try changing the input

Move one point and watch the midpoint move half as far in the same direction. Swapping the two points changes nothing: both formulas are symmetric.

What this calculator does

  • Finds the midpoint \(M\) of the segment \(P_1P_2\).
  • Finds the distance \(d\) between the points, in simplest radical form (such as \(2\sqrt{5}\)) and as a decimal.
  • Shows the horizontal and vertical changes \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) and the slope of the segment.
  • Draws the two points, the segment and its midpoint.

Coordinates can be whole numbers, decimals or fractions such as \(5/2\); all arithmetic is exact.

The geometry behind it

Draw a horizontal line through \(P_1\) and a vertical line through \(P_2\). They meet at a right angle, making a right triangle whose legs are \(|\Delta x|\) and \(|\Delta y|\) and whose hypotenuse is the segment \(P_1P_2\). The distance formula is the Pythagorean theorem applied to that triangle.

The midpoint is simpler still: halfway along the segment you have gone half of \(\Delta x\) across and half of \(\Delta y\) up, so each coordinate of \(M\) is the average of the matching coordinates.

Why squaring removes the sign

\(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) can be negative, but they are squared, so the order of the points does not matter: \((x_2 - x_1)^2 = (x_1 - x_2)^2\).

Deriving the formulas

Distance

The corner of the right triangle is \(Q(x_2, y_1)\). Then \(P_1Q = |x_2 - x_1|\) and \(QP_2 = |y_2 - y_1|\). By Pythagoras,

\[ d^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}. \]

Midpoint

A point a fraction \(t\) of the way from \(P_1\) to \(P_2\) is \(P_1 + t(P_2 - P_1)\). Halfway means \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\):

\[ x_M = x_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}(x_2 - x_1) = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \qquad y_M = \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}. \]

Other fractions give the section formula for dividing a segment in any ratio.

Complete worked example

Problem

Find the midpoint and the distance between \((-2, 1)\) and \((4, 9)\).

Step 1: Midpoint

\[ M = \left(\frac{-2 + 4}{2},\ \frac{1 + 9}{2}\right) = (1,\ 5) \]

Step 2: Changes in x and y

\[ \Delta x = 4 - (-2) = 6, \qquad \Delta y = 9 - 1 = 8 \]

Step 3: Distance

\[ d = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10 \]

Check

The midpoint should be half the distance from each end: from \((-2, 1)\) to \((1, 5)\) is \(\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\). ✓

Second worked example: classifying a triangle

Problem

Show that \(A(1, 2)\), \(B(4, 6)\) and \(C(8, 3)\) form a right isosceles triangle, and find the centre of the circle through all three points.

Step 1: The three side lengths

\[ AB = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5, \qquad BC = \sqrt{4^2 + (-3)^2} = 5, \qquad AC = \sqrt{7^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2} \]

Step 2: Test Pythagoras

\[ AB^2 + BC^2 = 25 + 25 = 50 = AC^2 \]

So the angle at \(B\) is 90°, and \(AB = BC\): the triangle is right and isosceles.

Step 3: Circumcentre

In a right triangle the hypotenuse is a diameter of the circumscribed circle, so the centre is the midpoint of \(AC\):

\[ M = \left(\frac{1 + 8}{2},\ \frac{2 + 3}{2}\right) = (4.5,\ 2.5) \]

Check

\[ MB = \sqrt{(-0.5)^2 + 3.5^2} = \sqrt{12.5} = \tfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{2} \]

That is half of \(AC\), as it should be. ✓

Practical applications

  • Surveying: the straight-line distance between two stations from their grid coordinates.
  • Structural drawings: member lengths in a truss from the coordinates of its joints.
  • Navigation and maps: distances on a local flat grid, and halfway meeting points.
  • Geometry proofs: showing that sides are equal, that diagonals bisect each other, or that a triangle is right-angled.

Common mistakes

  1. Subtracting in the midpoint formula. The midpoint adds the coordinates and halves them; the distance subtracts them.
  2. Sign errors with negatives. \(4 - (-2) = 6\), not 2. Put negative numbers in brackets.
  3. Taking the square root term by term. \(\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} \ne 6 + 8\).
  4. Mixing x and y. Pair \(x_1\) with \(x_2\) and \(y_1\) with \(y_2\), never \(x_1\) with \(y_2\).

Assumptions and limitations

  • Works in the two-dimensional Cartesian plane with the same units on both axes.
  • Inputs may be integers, decimals or fractions such as 3/4; all arithmetic is exact.
  • Distances are given in simplest radical form together with a decimal rounded to 6 places.
  • If both points are the same, the distance is 0 and the midpoint is that point.
  • For points in space, use the lines and planes in space calculator.

Frequently asked questions

What is the distance formula?

d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²). It is the Pythagorean theorem applied to the right triangle with legs Δx and Δy.

How do I find the midpoint of a segment?

Average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates: M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2).

How do I find an endpoint when I know the midpoint?

Double the midpoint and subtract the known endpoint: x₂ = 2x_M − x₁ and y₂ = 2y_M − y₁. For example, with M(3, −1) and A(−1, 4), the other end is (7, −6).

Does the order of the points matter?

No. The differences are squared in the distance formula and added in the midpoint formula, so swapping the points gives the same answers.
