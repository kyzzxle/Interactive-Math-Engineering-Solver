What the pigeonhole principle calculator does

The pigeonhole principle says that if you put more items into boxes than there are boxes, some box must hold at least two. More generally, n items in k boxes force some box to contain at least ⌈n/k⌉.

It sounds obvious, but it proves surprising facts: two people in London have exactly the same number of hairs; among any 13 people, two share a birth month.

Formulas and variables

\[ \text{some box holds at least } \left\lceil \frac{n}{k} \right\rceil \]\[ N = k(m - 1) + 1 \ \text{items guarantee } m \text{ in one box} \]
SymbolMeaning
\(n\)Number of items (pigeons)
\(k\)Number of boxes (holes)
\(\lceil \cdot \rceil\)Round up to the next whole number

Worked example

Socks in 4 colours: how many to guarantee 3 of one colour?

Step 1 — Worst case

You could take 2 of each colour — 8 socks — without 3 matching.

Step 2 — One more

\[ N = 4(3 - 1) + 1 = 9 \]

Step 3 — Conclusion

9 socks always contain 3 of the same colour, and 8 might not.

Domain, special cases and common mistakes

  • Whole numbers: at least one box. Up to 12 boxes are drawn.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing and rounding down instead of up.
  • Forgetting that the principle only proves existence — it does not say which box.
