What does it mean to factor a polynomial?

Factoring writes a polynomial as a product of simpler polynomials. It is the reverse of expanding. For example:

\[ x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6 = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3) \]

A factored form shows the roots at a glance: the polynomial is 0 exactly when one of its factors is 0.

Try changing the input

Enter \(x^4 + 4\). It has no real roots, yet it still factors into two quadratics: \((x^2 - 2x + 2)(x^2 + 2x + 2)\).

What this calculator does

  • Factors polynomials of degree 2 to 6 with rational coefficients, completely over the rationals.
  • Takes out the greatest common factor, finds every rational root with the rational root theorem, and divides each one out.
  • Finds remaining quadratic or cubic factors, and recognises patterns such as a difference of squares or a sum of cubes.
  • Checks the result by multiplying the factors back out.

The idea behind it

The factor theorem links roots and factors: \(P(r) = 0\) exactly when \((x - r)\) is a factor of \(P(x)\). So finding a root gives a factor, and dividing it out leaves a polynomial of lower degree to work on.

The rational root theorem says where to look: any rational root \(\tfrac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms has \(p\) dividing the constant term and \(q\) dividing the leading coefficient. That turns an infinite search into a short list.

Standard patterns

\(a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)\), \(a^3 \pm b^3 = (a \pm b)(a^2 \mp ab + b^2)\), and \(a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2 = (a \pm b)^2\).

Why the rational root theorem works

Suppose \(\tfrac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms is a root of \(a_nx^n + \dots + a_1x + a_0\). Substitute and multiply by \(q^n\):

\[ a_np^n + a_{n-1}p^{n-1}q + \dots + a_1pq^{n-1} + a_0q^n = 0. \]

Every term except the last contains \(p\), so \(p\) divides \(a_0q^n\); since \(p\) and \(q\) share no factor, \(p\) divides \(a_0\). In the same way, every term except the first contains \(q\), so \(q\) divides \(a_n\).

Complete worked example

Problem

Factor \(x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6\).

Step 1: Candidates

The leading coefficient is 1, so any rational root divides \(-6\): \(\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6\).

Step 2: Test

\[ P(1) = 1 - 6 + 11 - 6 = 0 \]

Step 3: Divide out (x − 1)

\[ x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6 = (x - 1)(x^2 - 5x + 6) \]

Step 4: Factor the quadratic

\[ x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3) \;\Rightarrow\; (x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3) \]

Check

Multiply the factors back out, two at a time:

\[ (x - 1)(x - 2) = x^2 - 3x + 2 \] \[ (x^2 - 3x + 2)(x - 3) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6\ \checkmark \]

Second worked example: GCF and patterns

Problem

Factor \(2x^4 - 32\) completely.

Step 1: Greatest common factor

\[ 2x^4 - 32 = 2(x^4 - 16) \]

Step 2: Difference of squares, twice

\[ x^4 - 16 = (x^2 - 4)(x^2 + 4) = (x - 2)(x + 2)(x^2 + 4) \]

Result

\[ 2x^4 - 32 = 2(x - 2)(x + 2)(x^2 + 4) \]

\(x^2 + 4\) has no real roots, so it does not factor further over the rationals (or the reals).

Practical applications

  • Solving equations: set each factor to 0.
  • Simplifying rational expressions and finding partial fractions.
  • Control systems: the roots of a characteristic polynomial decide stability.
  • Sketching graphs: roots and multiplicities show where and how a graph meets the x-axis.

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting the common factor. Take it out first; otherwise the factored form is not complete.
  2. Sign of the root. A root \(r = 3\) gives the factor \((x - 3)\), not \((x + 3)\).
  3. "Factoring" a sum of squares. \(x^2 + 4\) does not factor over the reals.
  4. Stopping too early. \(x^2 - 4\) inside a product still factors.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Degree 2 to 6, rational coefficients. Factoring is over the rationals: factors with irrational roots, such as \(x^2 - 2\), are left as they are.
  • All arithmetic is exact.

Frequently asked questions

How do I factor a cubic polynomial?

Find one root by testing the rational root candidates, divide out its factor with synthetic division, then factor the quadratic that remains.

What is the rational root theorem?

Any rational root p/q has p dividing the constant term and q dividing the leading coefficient.

What is the factor theorem?

P(r) = 0 if and only if (x − r) is a factor of P(x).

Can every polynomial be factored?

Over the complex numbers, every polynomial splits into linear factors. Over the rationals, some polynomials, like x² − 2, are irreducible.
