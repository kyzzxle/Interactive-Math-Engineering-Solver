What is a prismatoid?

A prismatoid is a solid whose vertices all lie in two parallel planes. Prisms, pyramids, frustums, wedges and obelisks (solids with two non-similar rectangular ends) are all prismatoids, and so is a section of an earth embankment between two survey stations.

What this calculator does

One formula, the prismoidal formula, gives the volume of all of them. It needs the areas of the two parallel ends and of the middle section halfway between them. The calculator:

  • accepts the three areas directly, for any prismatoid or earthwork,
  • or works them out for an obelisk (rectangular ends) or a wedge (top is an edge),
  • gives the lateral and total surface area for the obelisk and wedge,
  • compares the result with the common average-end-area shortcut.

The geometry behind it

Slice a prismatoid parallel to its ends. Each side of the slice moves linearly from one end to the other, so the area of the slice is a quadratic function of height. Simpson's rule integrates any quadratic exactly using its values at the start, the middle and the end, with weights 1, 4, 1. That is the prismoidal formula.

Average the sides, not the areas

For rectangular ends, the middle section is a rectangle whose sides are the averages of the end sides. Its area is not the average of the end areas, and that difference is exactly why the shortcut \(\tfrac{A_1 + A_2}{2}h\) is wrong.

Deriving the formulas

Let the section area at height \(y\) be \(A(y) = p + qy + ry^2\). Then

\[ V = \int_0^h A(y)\,dy = ph + \frac{qh^2}{2} + \frac{rh^3}{3}. \]

Now compute \(A_1 = A(0) = p\), \(A_m = A(h/2) = p + \tfrac{qh}{2} + \tfrac{rh^2}{4}\) and \(A_2 = A(h) = p + qh + rh^2\). Substituting,

\[ \frac{h}{6}\left(A_1 + 4A_m + A_2\right) = \frac{h}{6}\left(6p + 3qh + 2rh^2\right) = ph + \frac{qh^2}{2} + \frac{rh^3}{3} = V. \]

So the formula is exact whenever the cross-section is quadratic, which is the case for every prismatoid.

Complete worked example

Problem

An obelisk has a 12 m × 8 m base, a 6 m × 5 m top and a height of 5 m. Find its volume.

Step 1: End areas

\[ A_1 = 12 \times 8 = 96, \qquad A_2 = 6 \times 5 = 30 \]

Step 2: Middle section

\[ A_m = \frac{12 + 6}{2} \times \frac{8 + 5}{2} = 9 \times 6.5 = 58.5 \]

Step 3: Prismoidal formula

\[ V = \frac{5}{6}\left(96 + 4(58.5) + 30\right) = \frac{5}{6}(360) = 300\ \text{m}^3 \]

Compare

The average-end-area method gives \(\tfrac{96 + 30}{2}\times 5 = 315\) m³, which is 5% too high.

Second worked example: a wedge

Problem

A wedge has a 10 cm × 6 cm base and a top edge of 4 cm parallel to the 10 cm sides, 5 cm above the base. Find its volume.

Step 1: Sections

\[ A_1 = 60, \qquad A_m = \frac{10 + 4}{2}\cdot\frac{6}{2} = 7 \times 3 = 21, \qquad A_2 = 0 \]

Step 2: Volume

\[ V = \frac{5}{6}\left(60 + 4(21) + 0\right) = \frac{5}{6}(144) = 120\ \text{cm}^3 \]

Check with the wedge formula: \(\tfrac{6 \times 5}{6}(2 \times 10 + 4) = 5 \times 24 = 120\) ✓.

Practical applications

  • Earthworks. Highway engineers find cut-and-fill volumes between survey stations with the prismoidal formula.
  • Stockpiles and embankments on rectangular footprints.
  • Concrete and masonry. Tapered footings and plinths with different slopes on each side.
  • Roof spaces such as wedge-shaped attics.

Common mistakes

  1. Averaging the end areas for Aₘ. Average the side lengths instead.
  2. Forgetting the 4. The middle section is weighted four times.
  3. Dividing by 3 instead of 6. The frustum formula uses \(h/3\); the prismoidal formula uses \(h/6\).
  4. Sloping height. \(h\) is perpendicular to the ends.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The two ends are parallel and \(h\) is measured perpendicular to them.
  • The surface areas for the obelisk and wedge assume the top is centred over the base. The volume does not depend on that.
  • For a solid with similar ends, the frustum of a pyramid calculator gives the same volume with \(\tfrac{h}{3}(A_1 + A_2 + \sqrt{A_1 A_2})\).
  • When entering three areas, the middle section of a convex solid can never be smaller than \(\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{A_1} + \sqrt{A_2}}{2}\right)^2\); smaller values are rejected.

Frequently asked questions

What is the prismoidal formula?

V = (h/6)(A₁ + 4Aₘ + A₂), where A₁ and A₂ are the end areas, Aₘ is the middle section and h is the distance between the ends.

Is the middle section the average of the end areas?

No. For rectangular ends, average the lengths and the widths separately and multiply them.

Does the prismoidal formula work for a cone or sphere?

Yes, because their cross-sections are also quadratic in height. For a sphere, A₁ = A₂ = 0 and Aₘ = πr², giving (2r/6)(4πr²) = (4/3)πr³.

What is the difference between a prismatoid and a frustum?

A frustum is a special prismatoid whose two ends are similar. A prismatoid's ends can be any shapes.
