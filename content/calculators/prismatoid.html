What the prismatoid calculator does

A prismatoid is a solid whose vertices all lie in two parallel planes. Prisms, pyramids, frustums, wedges and obelisks (solids with two non-similar rectangular ends) are all prismatoids, and so is a section of an earth embankment between two survey stations.

One formula gives the volume of all of them: the prismoidal formula. It needs the areas of the two parallel ends and of the middle section, the cross-section halfway between them. The calculator accepts the three areas directly, or works them out for an obelisk or a wedge, and also gives the lateral and total surface area for those shapes.

Try it

In the explorer, make the two ends very different, such as a long narrow top on a wide base. The "average end area" estimate drifts away from the true volume, while the prismoidal formula stays exact.

Formulas and variables

\[ V = \frac{h}{6}\left(A_1 + 4A_m + A_2\right) \]
\[ \text{Obelisk: } A_m = \frac{L_1 + L_2}{2}\cdot\frac{W_1 + W_2}{2} \qquad \text{Wedge: } V = \frac{w\,h}{6}\,(2l + e) \]
SymbolMeaning
\(A_1,\ A_2\)Areas of the two parallel ends
\(A_m\)Area of the section halfway between the ends
\(h\)Perpendicular distance between the ends
\(L, W\)Lengths and widths of rectangular ends
\(l, w, e\)Base length and width of a wedge, and its top edge (parallel to \(l\))

For rectangular ends the middle section is a rectangle whose sides are the averages of the sides, not a rectangle with the average area. That difference is exactly what makes the prismoidal formula correct where the average-end-area method is not. The formula is Simpson's rule applied to the cross-section area, so it is exact whenever that area is a quadratic function of height.

Worked example

An obelisk with a 12 m × 8 m base, a 6 m × 5 m top and a height of 5 m

Step 1: End areas

\[ A_1 = 12 \times 8 = 96, \qquad A_2 = 6 \times 5 = 30 \]

Step 2: Middle section

\[ A_m = \frac{12 + 6}{2} \times \frac{8 + 5}{2} = 9 \times 6.5 = 58.5 \]

Step 3: Prismoidal formula

\[ V = \frac{5}{6}\left(96 + 4(58.5) + 30\right) = \frac{5}{6}(360) = 300\ \text{m}^3 \]

The average-end-area method would give \(\tfrac{96 + 30}{2} \times 5 = 315\ \text{m}^3\), which is 5% too high.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The two ends are parallel and \(h\) is measured perpendicular to them.
  • The surface areas for the obelisk and wedge assume the top is centred over the base. The volume does not depend on that.
  • For a solid with similar ends, the frustum of a pyramid calculator gives the same volume with \(\tfrac{h}{3}(A_1 + A_2 + \sqrt{A_1 A_2})\).
  • When entering three areas, the middle section of a convex solid can never be smaller than \(\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{A_1} + \sqrt{A_2}}{2}\right)^2\); smaller values are rejected.
