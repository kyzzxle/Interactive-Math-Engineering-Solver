Indefinite integrals (antiderivatives)

An antiderivative of \(f\) is a function \(F\) whose derivative is \(f\). The indefinite integral \(\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C\) represents all of them at once, since adding any constant \(C\) does not change the derivative. This calculator integrates term by term using a table of standard rules, shows which rule each term needs, and checks its answer by differentiating it.

Try changing the input

Integrate \(\cos(2x)\) and then \(\cos(5x)\): the answer gains a factor \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) or \(\tfrac{1}{5}\) because of the inner linear function — the reverse of the chain rule.

Here \(u = ax + b\) is linear. Sums and constant multiples of these are integrated term by term; polynomials in factored form are expanded first.

Worked example

\(\int \left(3x^2 + 4\cos 2x - \frac{1}{x}\right) dx\)

Step 1 — Term by term

\[ \int 3x^2\,dx = x^3, \qquad \int 4\cos 2x\,dx = 2\sin 2x, \qquad \int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x| \]

Step 2 — Combine and add C

\[ x^3 + 2\sin 2x - \ln|x| + C \]

Step 3 — Check

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[x^3 + 2\sin 2x - \ln|x|\right] = 3x^2 + 4\cos 2x - \frac{1}{x} \;\checkmark \]

Limitations

  • Only the forms in the table are supported. Integrals that need substitution with a non-linear inner function, integration by parts (such as \(\int x\sin x\,dx\)) or partial fractions are reported as unsupported rather than guessed.
  • Some functions, such as \(e^{-x^2}\), have no elementary antiderivative at all; use the definite integral calculator for their numerical values.
  • Every answer is verified by differentiating it and comparing with the integrand at several points.
