What is a rational expression?

A rational expression is a fraction whose numerator and denominator are polynomials, such as \(\tfrac{x^2 - 9}{x^2 + x - 6}\). It is simplified exactly like a numerical fraction: factor the top and the bottom and cancel the common factors. The one new idea is that the expression is not defined where the denominator is 0, and those excluded values stay excluded even after cancelling.

Try changing the input

Enter \(\tfrac{x + 1}{x^2 + 1}\). The top and bottom share no factor, so it is already in lowest terms, and \(x^2 + 1\) is never 0, so nothing is excluded.

What this calculator does

  • Factors the numerator and denominator over the rationals.
  • Finds their greatest common factor with Euclid's algorithm and cancels it.
  • Lists the excluded values, the holes (cancelled zeros) and the vertical asymptotes.
  • Checks the simplification at sample points and graphs the result.

The idea behind it

\(\tfrac{AC}{BC} = \tfrac{A}{B}\) whenever \(C \ne 0\): dividing the top and the bottom by the same non-zero factor does not change the value. But only factors cancel. In \(\tfrac{x + 3}{x + 5}\) the \(x\) is part of a sum and cannot be cancelled.

A cancelled factor \((x - a)\) leaves a hole in the graph at \(x = a\): the simplified formula has a value there, but the original expression does not. A factor that stays in the denominator gives a vertical asymptote.

Equal, except at the holes

\(\tfrac{x^2 - 9}{x + 3}\) and \(x - 3\) agree for every \(x\) except \(x = -3\), where the first is not defined.

Formula breakdown

\[ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = \frac{P(x) / G(x)}{Q(x) / G(x)}, \qquad G = \gcd(P, Q) \]

Excluded valuesZeros of the original denominator \(Q\)
HolesZeros of the common factor \(G\)

Euclid's algorithm for polynomials

To find \(\gcd(P, Q)\) without factoring, divide \(P\) by \(Q\) and keep the remainder \(R\). Then \(\gcd(P, Q) = \gcd(Q, R)\), because any common factor of \(P\) and \(Q\) also divides \(R = P - \text{(quotient)}\,Q\). Repeat until the remainder is 0; the last non-zero remainder is the gcd. This always works, even when the factors have irrational roots.

Complete worked example

Problem

Simplify \(\dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x^2 + x - 6}\) and describe its graph.

Step 1: Factor

\[ \frac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{(x + 3)(x - 2)} \]

Step 2: Cancel the common factor

\[ \frac{x - 3}{x - 2}, \qquad x \ne -3,\ x \ne 2 \]

Step 3: Features

Hole at \(x = -3\), at height \(\tfrac{-6}{-5} = \tfrac{6}{5}\). Vertical asymptote \(x = 2\). x-intercept at \(x = 3\).

Check

At \(x = 0\): original \(\tfrac{-9}{-6} = 1.5\), simplified \(\tfrac{-3}{-2} = 1.5\). ✓

Second worked example: a cubic over a quadratic

Problem

Simplify \(\dfrac{x^3 - 1}{x^2 - 1}\).

Step 1: Factor (difference of cubes and of squares)

\[ \frac{(x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1)}{(x - 1)(x + 1)} \]

Step 2: Cancel

\[ \frac{x^2 + x + 1}{x + 1}, \qquad x \ne 1,\ x \ne -1 \]

Step 3: Features

Hole at \(x = 1\), at height \(\tfrac{3}{2}\). Vertical asymptote \(x = -1\). The factor \(x^2 + x + 1\) has no real roots, so there are no x-intercepts.

Practical applications

  • Transfer functions: in control engineering, cancelling factors shows the poles and zeros of a system.
  • Average cost: total cost divided by quantity is a rational function.
  • Optics and circuits: lens and parallel-resistance formulas simplify to rational expressions.
  • Calculus: simplifying before taking a limit removes a 0/0 form.

Common mistakes

  1. Cancelling terms instead of factors. \(\tfrac{x + 3}{x + 5} \ne \tfrac{3}{5}\).
  2. Dropping the excluded values. They come from the original denominator, before cancelling.
  3. Confusing holes with asymptotes. A cancelled factor gives a hole; a remaining one gives an asymptote.
  4. Sign slips with \((a - x)\). \(a - x = -(x - a)\), so it cancels with \((x - a)\) leaving \(-1\).

Assumptions and limitations

  • Numerator and denominator are polynomials in \(x\) of degree up to 6, with rational coefficients.
  • Factoring is over the rationals; irrational zeros are found numerically and marked as rounded.

Frequently asked questions

How do I simplify a rational expression?

Factor the numerator and the denominator completely, then cancel the factors they have in common.

What are excluded values?

Values of x that make the original denominator 0. The expression is not defined there.

What is the difference between a hole and a vertical asymptote?

A hole comes from a factor that cancels; the graph is missing one point. An asymptote comes from a factor left in the denominator; the graph shoots off to infinity.

Can I cancel x in (x + 2)/x?

No. The x on top is part of the sum x + 2, not a factor of the whole numerator.
