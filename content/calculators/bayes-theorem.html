What the Bayes’ theorem calculator does

Conditional probability \(P(A \mid B)\) is the probability of A once we know B happened. Bayes’ theorem turns it around: from how likely the evidence B is when A is true, it gives how likely A is when we see B.

It is essential for medical tests, spam filters and any situation where a rare condition must be inferred from imperfect evidence — and it often gives surprising answers.

Try it

With a 1% prevalence, a 95%-accurate test is right only about 16% of the times it says “positive”.

Formulas and variables

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \]\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^\prime)\,P(A^\prime)} \]
SymbolMeaning
\(P(A)\)Prior: how likely A is before the evidence
\(P(B \mid A),\ P(B \mid A^\prime)\)Likelihoods: how likely the evidence is if A is true, or false
\(P(A \mid B)\)Posterior: how likely A is after seeing B

Worked example

A disease affects 1% of people; the test detects 95% of cases and gives 5% false positives

Step 1 — Total probability of a positive test

\[ P(+) = 0.95(0.01) + 0.05(0.99) = 0.059 \]

Step 2 — Bayes’ theorem

\[ P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.95(0.01)}{0.059} \approx 0.161 \]

Step 3 — Interpret

Only about 16% of people who test positive have the disease, because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones.

Domain, special cases and common mistakes

  • Probabilities between 0 and 1 (or 0% to 100%). P(B) must not be 0.
  • P(A and B) cannot exceed P(B).

Common mistakes

  • Confusing P(B | A) with P(A | B) (the prosecutor’s fallacy).
  • Ignoring the prior when the condition is rare.
