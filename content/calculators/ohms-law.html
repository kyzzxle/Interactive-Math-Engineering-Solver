What the Ohm's law calculator does

Ohm's law links the voltage across a resistor, the current through it and its resistance. Voltage is the "push" that drives charge around a circuit, current is the rate at which charge flows, and resistance is how strongly the component opposes that flow. Know any two and you can find the third.

The calculator also finds the power the resistor turns into heat, and draws the circuit with the values labelled.

Try it

In the explorer, double the voltage: the current doubles too. Then increase the resistance and watch the I–V line become flatter.

Formulas and variables

\[ V = I\,R \qquad I = \frac{V}{R} \qquad R = \frac{V}{I} \]
\[ P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R} \]
SymbolMeaningSI unit
\(V\)Voltage (potential difference)volts, V
\(I\)Currentamperes, A (1 mA = 0.001 A)
\(R\)Resistanceohms, Ω
\(P\)Power dissipatedwatts, W

Worked example

A 9 V battery across a 450 Ω resistor

Step 1 — Known values

\(V = 9\) V, \(R = 450\) Ω.

Step 2 — Current

\[ I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{9}{450} = 0.02\ \text{A} = 20\ \text{mA} \]

Step 3 — Power

\[ P = V I = 9 \times 0.02 = 0.18\ \text{W} \]

A standard 0.25 W resistor would handle this, but only just — designers usually allow a safety margin.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The resistor is ohmic: its resistance stays the same whatever the current. Filament lamps, diodes and LEDs do not obey Ohm's law.
  • Temperature is constant. Real resistances change as components heat up.
  • Wires and the power supply are ideal (no internal resistance). For several resistors, use the series and parallel calculator first.
  • Values are direct-current (DC) magnitudes and must not be negative; resistance must be greater than 0.
