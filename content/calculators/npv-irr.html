What the NPV and IRR calculator does

An investment usually costs money now and returns money later. To judge it fairly, every cash flow is moved to the same point in time, today, by discounting it at the minimum attractive rate of return (MARR). The sum is the net present worth or net present value (NPV). If it is positive, the investment earns more than the MARR.

The calculator discounts any series of cash flows, one per period, and reports the NPV, the internal rate of return (IRR), the simple payback period and a table of every discounted flow. It also draws the cash-flow diagram and the NPV curve.

Try it

In the explorer, drag the interest rate up. The NPV falls as later returns are discounted more heavily, and crosses zero at the IRR. Any MARR to the left of that crossing makes the project worth doing.

Formulas and variables

\[ NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1 + i)^t} = C_0 + \frac{C_1}{1 + i} + \frac{C_2}{(1 + i)^2} + \cdots + \frac{C_n}{(1 + i)^n} \]
\[ \text{IRR } i^*: \quad \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1 + i^*)^t} = 0 \]
SymbolMeaning
\(C_t\)Net cash flow in period \(t\): negative for money spent, positive for money received
\(i\)Interest rate (MARR) per period
\(n\)Number of periods
\(i^*\)Internal rate of return: the rate at which NPV is exactly 0

The IRR has no formula in general. The calculator finds it numerically, by locating where the NPV changes sign and narrowing the interval by bisection.

Worked example

An investment of 100,000 returns 30,000, 35,000, 40,000 and 45,000 over four years. Is it worth it at a MARR of 10%?

Step 1: Discount each return

\[ \frac{30{,}000}{1.1} + \frac{35{,}000}{1.1^2} + \frac{40{,}000}{1.1^3} + \frac{45{,}000}{1.1^4} \approx 27{,}272.73 + 28{,}925.62 + 30{,}052.59 + 30{,}735.61 \]

Step 2: Net present worth

\[ NPV \approx 116{,}986.54 - 100{,}000 = 16{,}986.54 \]

Step 3: Decision

The NPV is positive, so the project earns more than 10%. Its IRR is about 17.09%, and the simple payback is about 2.9 years.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Cash flows occur at the end of each period, and the rate is per period (yearly flows need a yearly rate).
  • When the cash flows change sign more than once, there can be more than one IRR, or none. The NPV is always well defined, so use it to decide.
  • The payback period ignores interest and any cash flows after payback, so it is only a rough guide.
  • For equal yearly flows, the annuity calculator gives the same present worth with a single factor.
