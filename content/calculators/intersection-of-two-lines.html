What is the intersection of two lines?

Two lines in a plane either cross at exactly one point, never meet because they are parallel, or are the same line. The intersection point is the one pair \((x, y)\) that satisfies both equations at once, so finding it means solving a system of two linear equations.

Try changing the input

Make the second line a multiple of the first, for example \(2x + 4y - 8 = 0\) and \(x + 2y - 4 = 0\). The determinant becomes 0 and the calculator reports that the lines coincide.

What this calculator does

  • Takes two lines in general form \(A_1x + B_1y + C_1 = 0\) and \(A_2x + B_2y + C_2 = 0\).
  • Finds the intersection point exactly with Cramer's rule, and checks it in both equations.
  • Detects parallel and coincident lines.
  • Gives the acute angle between the lines and draws both of them.

The geometry behind it

Each equation describes every point of one line. A point on both lines must satisfy both equations, so the intersection is the solution of the system. Whether such a point exists depends only on the directions of the lines:

  • Different slopes: exactly one solution.
  • Equal slopes, different positions: no solution (parallel lines).
  • One equation a multiple of the other: infinitely many solutions (the same line).

The determinant test

\(D = A_1B_2 - A_2B_1\) is zero exactly when the slopes \(-A_1/B_1\) and \(-A_2/B_2\) are equal. So \(D \ne 0\) means one intersection point.

Formula breakdown

\[ D = A_1B_2 - A_2B_1, \qquad x = \frac{B_1C_2 - B_2C_1}{D}, \qquad y = \frac{A_2C_1 - A_1C_2}{D} \]
CaseConditionResult
Intersecting\(D \ne 0\)One point, from the formulas above
Parallel\(D = 0\), equations not multiplesNo intersection
Coincident\(D = 0\), equations multiplesEvery point of the line
Perpendicular\(A_1A_2 + B_1B_2 = 0\)The lines meet at 90°

Variables explained

SymbolMeaning
\(A_1, B_1, C_1\)Coefficients of the first line.
\(A_2, B_2, C_2\)Coefficients of the second line.
\(D\)Determinant of the coefficients of \(x\) and \(y\).
\((x, y)\)The intersection point.

Deriving the formulas

Write the system as \(A_1x + B_1y = -C_1\) and \(A_2x + B_2y = -C_2\). To eliminate \(y\), multiply the first equation by \(B_2\), the second by \(B_1\), and subtract:

\[ (A_1B_2 - A_2B_1)\,x = -C_1B_2 + C_2B_1 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{B_1C_2 - B_2C_1}{A_1B_2 - A_2B_1}. \]

Eliminating \(x\) the same way gives \(y = \dfrac{A_2C_1 - A_1C_2}{A_1B_2 - A_2B_1}\). The shared denominator is the determinant \(D\); this is Cramer's rule for a 2 × 2 system.

The same answer comes from substitution (solve one equation for \(y\) and substitute) or from elimination by hand. Cramer's rule simply packages elimination into one formula.

Complete worked example

Problem

Find where \(2x + 3y - 12 = 0\) meets \(x - y - 1 = 0\).

Step 1: Determinant

\[ D = (2)(-1) - (1)(3) = -5 \ne 0 \]

The determinant is not zero, so there is exactly one intersection point.

Step 2: Cramer's rule

\[ x = \frac{(3)(-1) - (-1)(-12)}{-5} = \frac{-15}{-5} = 3, \qquad y = \frac{(1)(-12) - (2)(-1)}{-5} = \frac{-10}{-5} = 2 \]

Step 3: Check in both equations

\[ 2(3) + 3(2) - 12 = 0 \ \checkmark \qquad 3 - 2 - 1 = 0 \ \checkmark \]

The lines meet at \((3, 2)\). Their slopes are \(-\tfrac{2}{3}\) and \(1\), so the acute angle between them is \(\tan^{-1} 5 \approx 78.69^\circ\).

Second worked example: substitution and a parallel pair

Part 1: Lines in slope–intercept form

Find where \(y = 2x - 1\) meets \(y = -x + 5\).

Both expressions equal \(y\), so set them equal: \(2x - 1 = -x + 5\), giving \(3x = 6\) and \(x = 2\). Then \(y = 2(2) - 1 = 3\). The intersection is \((2, 3)\).

In general form these are \(2x - y - 1 = 0\) and \(x + y - 5 = 0\), and Cramer's rule gives the same point: \(D = 3\), \(x = \tfrac{6}{3} = 2\), \(y = \tfrac{9}{3} = 3\).

Part 2: A pair with no solution

For \(x + 2y - 4 = 0\) and \(2x + 4y + 3 = 0\):

\[ D = (1)(4) - (2)(2) = 0 \]

Both lines have slope \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\), but doubling the first equation gives \(2x + 4y - 8 = 0\), not \(+3\). They are parallel and never meet.

Practical applications

  • Break-even analysis: where a cost line meets a revenue line (see the break-even calculator).
  • Surveying: locating a point from two bearings drawn from known stations.
  • Road layout: where two straight alignments cross.
  • Computer graphics: clipping and collision tests between line segments.

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting to move C. Read \(C\) from the form \(Ax + By + C = 0\). For \(2x + 3y = 12\), \(C = -12\).
  2. Mixing up the numerators. The order of the products matters; a slip changes the sign of \(x\) or \(y\).
  3. Dividing by a zero determinant. If \(D = 0\), stop: the lines are parallel or the same.
  4. Skipping the check. Substituting the answer into both equations catches almost every arithmetic slip.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Both lines are entered in general form; \(A\) and \(B\) cannot both be 0 in the same line.
  • Coordinates are exact fractions; decimals are shown rounded to 6 places.
  • For three or more equations, use the systems of linear equations solver.
  • Lines in space may be skew (not parallel, yet never meeting); this calculator covers lines in a plane.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find where two lines intersect?

Solve the two equations together, by substitution, elimination or Cramer's rule. The solution (x, y) is the intersection point.

How can I tell if two lines are parallel?

Their slopes are equal, which in general form means A₁B₂ − A₂B₁ = 0. If the equations are not multiples of each other, the lines never meet.

What does it mean if the lines coincide?

The two equations describe the same line, so every point on it is shared and there are infinitely many intersection points.

What is Cramer's rule?

A formula that solves a linear system with determinants. For two lines, x = (B₁C₂ − B₂C₁)/D and y = (A₂C₁ − A₁C₂)/D with D = A₁B₂ − A₂B₁.
