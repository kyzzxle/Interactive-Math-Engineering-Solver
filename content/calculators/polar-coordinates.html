What are polar coordinates?

Rectangular coordinates locate a point by how far it is across and up, \((x, y)\). Polar coordinates locate it by how far it is from a fixed point, the pole (the origin), and in which direction: \((r, \theta)\), where \(r\) is the distance and \(\theta\) is the angle measured anticlockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis (the polar axis).

Curves with rotational patterns, such as flowers, spirals and heart shapes, have very simple polar equations.

What this calculator does

  • Converts a rectangular point to polar form, explaining the quadrant, with exact values for special angles.
  • Converts a polar point to rectangular form.
  • Graphs roses, cardioids, limaçons, lemniscates, circles and spirals, and gives their shape, symmetry, largest \(r\) and enclosed area.
  • Finds the area swept between two angles with \(\tfrac{1}{2}\int r^2\,d\theta\).

The geometry behind it

Draw a right triangle from the pole to the point: the hypotenuse is \(r\), the horizontal leg is \(x\) and the vertical leg is \(y\). Trigonometry links the two systems: \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), and \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\).

One point, many names

\((r, \theta)\), \((r, \theta + 360^\circ)\) and \((-r, \theta + 180^\circ)\) all describe the same point. A negative \(r\) means "go the opposite way from the direction \(\theta\)".

Deriving the formulas

Area in polar coordinates

A thin wedge between \(\theta\) and \(\theta + d\theta\) is almost a sector of a circle of radius \(r\), whose area is \(\tfrac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta\). Adding the wedges:

\[ A = \frac{1}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta. \]

A rose petal

For \(r = 4\cos 3\theta\), one petal runs from \(\theta = -30^\circ\) to \(30^\circ\) (where \(r = 0\)):

\[ A = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi/6}^{\pi/6} 16\cos^2 3\theta\,d\theta = 8\int_{-\pi/6}^{\pi/6}\frac{1 + \cos 6\theta}{2}\,d\theta = \frac{4\pi}{3}. \]

Three petals give \(4\pi\), which is \(\tfrac{\pi a^2}{4}\) with \(a = 4\).

Complete worked example

Problem

Convert \((-3, 3)\) to polar coordinates.

Step 1: Distance

\[ r = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.243 \]

Step 2: Reference angle

\[ \tan\alpha = \left|\frac{3}{-3}\right| = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha = 45^\circ \]

Step 3: Quadrant

The point is in quadrant II, so \(\theta = 180^\circ - 45^\circ = 135^\circ = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\).

Answer

\(\left(3\sqrt{2},\ 135^\circ\right)\). A calculator's \(\tan^{-1}(-1)\) would give −45°, which points into quadrant IV: always check the quadrant.

Second worked example: polar to rectangular and a cardioid

Polar to rectangular

\[ (6, 150^\circ):\quad x = 6\cos 150^\circ = 6\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = -3\sqrt{3},\quad y = 6\sin 150^\circ = 3 \]

Area of a cardioid

For \(r = 2(1 + \cos\theta)\):

\[ A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} 4(1 + \cos\theta)^2\,d\theta = 2\int_0^{2\pi}\left(1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta\right)d\theta = 2(2\pi + 0 + \pi) = 6\pi \]

Practical applications

  • Radar and navigation: targets are located by distance and bearing.
  • Engineering: microphone and antenna pickup patterns are cardioids and roses.
  • Physics: orbits and fields with circular symmetry.
  • Complex numbers: the polar form \(r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\).

Common mistakes

  1. Trusting tan⁻¹ blindly. It only returns −90° to 90°; points in quadrants II and III need 180° added.
  2. Degrees in integrals. \(\tfrac{1}{2}\int r^2\,d\theta\) needs \(\theta\) in radians.
  3. Counting rose petals. Odd \(n\) gives \(n\) petals, even \(n\) gives \(2n\).
  4. Double-counting loops. Integrating a limaçon with an inner loop over 0 to 2π counts the inner loop twice.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Angles are entered in degrees; integrals use radians internally.
  • The area between two angles is the area swept by the radius from the pole.
  • The origin has no defined polar angle.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert rectangular to polar coordinates?

r = √(x² + y²) and θ = tan⁻¹(y/x), adjusted for the quadrant of the point.

How do I convert polar to rectangular coordinates?

x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ.

How many petals does r = a cos nθ have?

n petals when n is odd and 2n petals when n is even.

How do I find the area of a polar curve?

Use A = ½∫ r² dθ between the angles that bound the region, with θ in radians.
