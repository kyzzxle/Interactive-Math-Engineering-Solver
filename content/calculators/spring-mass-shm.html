What the spring-mass SHM calculator does

A mass on a spring, pulled and released, oscillates in simple harmonic motion: the spring’s force is proportional to the displacement (Hooke’s law), so the motion is a perfect sine or cosine wave.

The period depends only on the mass and the spring constant — not on how far you pull it. Energy swaps continuously between kinetic energy (fastest at the middle) and elastic potential energy (largest at the ends).

Formulas and variables

\[ F = -kx \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \]\[ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t) \qquad v_{\max} = A\omega \qquad E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 \]
SymbolMeaning
\(m\)Mass
\(k\)Spring constant
\(A\)Amplitude
\(\omega,\ T\)Angular frequency and period

Worked example

A 0.5 kg mass on a 20 N/m spring, amplitude 0.1 m

Step 1 — Angular frequency

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{20}{0.5}} \approx 6.32\ \text{rad/s} \]

Step 2 — Period

\[ T = \frac{2\pi}{6.32} \approx 0.994\ \text{s} \]

Step 3 — Maximum speed and energy

\[ v_{\max} = 0.1 \times 6.32 \approx 0.632\ \text{m/s},\quad E = \tfrac{1}{2}(20)(0.1)^2 = 0.1\ \text{J} \]

Domain, special cases and common mistakes

  • Ideal massless spring, no friction, small enough amplitude to obey Hooke’s law.
  • The mass starts at rest at x = A (cosine motion).

Common mistakes

  • Using T = 2π√(k/m) (the ratio is upside down).
  • Thinking a bigger amplitude means a longer period.
