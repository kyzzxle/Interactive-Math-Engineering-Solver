What is a hyperbola?

A hyperbola is the set of all points whose distances to two fixed points, the foci, differ by the same constant. It has two separate branches, each curving around one focus, and far from the centre both branches straighten out along two crossing lines called asymptotes.

Try changing the input

Make \(a = b\). The asymptotes become \(y = \pm x\), at right angles to each other: a rectangular hyperbola with eccentricity \(\sqrt{2}\).

What this calculator does

Choose whether the hyperbola opens left and right or up and down, then enter the centre \((h, k)\), the transverse semi-axis \(a\) and the conjugate semi-axis \(b\). It returns:

  • the standard and general forms of the equation;
  • the vertices, foci and focal distance \(c\);
  • the equations of the asymptotes;
  • the eccentricity and the latus rectum;
  • a graph with the asymptotes and foci.

The geometry behind it

The transverse axis joins the two vertices and has length \(2a\); the difference of the focal distances is always \(2a\). The conjugate axis, of length \(2b\), is perpendicular to it. Draw a rectangle \(2a\) by \(2b\) around the centre: its diagonals are the asymptotes, and its half-diagonal is \(c\):

\[ c^2 = a^2 + b^2. \]

Because \(c > a\), the eccentricity \(e = c/a\) is always greater than 1. The larger \(e\), the wider the branches open.

Ellipse versus hyperbola

Ellipse: the distances to the foci add to a constant, \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\), \(e \lt 1\). Hyperbola: they differ by a constant, \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), \(e > 1\).

Deriving the equation and the asymptotes

Place the foci at \((\pm c, 0)\) and require the distances to differ by \(2a\):

\[ \left| \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} - \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} \right| = 2a. \]

Squaring twice, as for the ellipse, gives \((c^2 - a^2)x^2 - a^2y^2 = a^2(c^2 - a^2)\). With \(b^2 = c^2 - a^2\) this is \(\tfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\).

Asymptotes

Solve for \(y\): \(y = \pm\tfrac{b}{a}\sqrt{x^2 - a^2} = \pm\tfrac{b}{a}\,x\sqrt{1 - a^2/x^2}\). As \(|x|\) grows, the square root approaches 1, so the curve approaches the lines \(y = \pm\tfrac{b}{a}x\).

Complete worked example

Problem

A hyperbola opens left and right, with centre \((1, -2)\), \(a = 3\) and \(b = 4\). Find its equation, vertices, foci and asymptotes.

Step 1: Standard form

\[ \frac{(x - 1)^2}{9} - \frac{(y + 2)^2}{16} = 1 \]

Step 2: Focal distance

\[ c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25, \qquad c = 5 \]

Step 3: Vertices and foci

\[ \text{vertices } (-2, -2),\ (4, -2), \qquad \text{foci } (-4, -2),\ (6, -2) \]

Step 4: Asymptotes and shape

\[ y + 2 = \pm\tfrac{4}{3}(x - 1), \qquad e = \tfrac{5}{3}, \qquad \text{latus rectum } \tfrac{2(16)}{3} = \tfrac{32}{3} \]

Step 5: General form

Multiply by 144 and expand: \(16x^2 - 9y^2 - 32x - 36y - 164 = 0\).

Check

The vertex \((4, -2)\) is 8 from the focus \((-4, -2)\) and 2 from \((6, -2)\). The difference is \(6 = 2a\). ✓

Second worked example: from vertices and asymptotes

Problem

A hyperbola has vertices \((0, \pm 2)\) and asymptotes \(y = \pm 2x\). Find its equation and foci.

Step 1: Direction and \(a\)

The vertices are on the y-axis, so it opens up and down with centre \((0, 0)\) and \(a = 2\).

Step 2: Find \(b\) from the asymptotes

For this direction the asymptote slopes are \(\pm\tfrac{a}{b}\), so \(\tfrac{2}{b} = 2\) and \(b = 1\).

Step 3: Equation and foci

\[ \frac{y^2}{4} - x^2 = 1, \qquad c = \sqrt{4 + 1} = \sqrt{5}, \qquad \text{foci } \left(0, \pm\sqrt{5}\right) \]

The eccentricity is \(\tfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.118\).

Practical applications

  • Navigation (LORAN, GPS): a constant difference in signal arrival times from two stations places the receiver on a hyperbola.
  • Cooling towers: their curved profile is a hyperboloid, which is strong and can be built from straight members.
  • Optics: hyperbolic mirrors in Cassegrain telescopes.
  • Physics: the path of a comet that passes the Sun once and never returns, and Boyle's law \(PV = \text{constant}\).

Common mistakes

  1. Using \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\). That is the ellipse. For a hyperbola, add.
  2. Assuming \(a > b\). In a hyperbola \(a\) goes with the positive squared term, whichever is larger.
  3. Wrong asymptote slope. It is \(\tfrac{b}{a}\) when the hyperbola opens left and right, but \(\tfrac{a}{b}\) when it opens up and down.
  4. Forgetting the centre. The asymptotes pass through \((h, k)\), not the origin.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The axes are parallel to the coordinate axes. Rotated hyperbolas such as \(xy = 4\) are handled by the general conic calculator.
  • \(a\) and \(b\) must be positive. Fractions are allowed and the results are exact.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find the foci of a hyperbola?

Compute c² = a² + b². The foci are c units from the centre along the transverse axis.

How do I find the asymptotes?

They pass through the centre with slopes ±b/a for a hyperbola opening left and right, or ±a/b for one opening up and down.

Why is the eccentricity of a hyperbola greater than 1?

Because c² = a² + b² makes c larger than a, so e = c/a > 1.

What is a rectangular hyperbola?

One with a = b. Its asymptotes are perpendicular and its eccentricity is √2.
